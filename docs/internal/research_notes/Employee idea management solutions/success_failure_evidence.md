# Why employee suggestion systems, idea programs and "employee voice" channels succeed or fail: evidence and design implications

Source-type labels used throughout: **[PR]** peer-reviewed, **[S]** seminal or older work (pre-2015), **[B]** practitioner book or author article, **[V]** vendor survey or vendor blog (commercial interest, not independently verified), **[M]** media or secondary summary. "(snippet)" means the figure came from a search-result summary because the page could not be fetched. Verify those before quoting them on a slide.

## 1. Why people stop speaking up: silence, futility, and how managers' responses shape future voice

### Takeaway
Research identifies two separate drivers. **Fear** (low psychological safety) produces silence. **Perceived futility**, the belief that speaking up changes nothing, suppresses voice. Of the two, the *perceived impact* of speaking up is the stronger predictor of whether people voice ideas. The owner's worry ("the team will stop proposing anything") matches the documented pattern: when managers don't respond, voice dries up and turns into exit (turnover).

### Cited Findings
- **[S][PR] Morrison & Milliken (2000), AMR.** Coined "organizational silence" as a collective phenomenon in which employees widely withhold information about problems. They attribute it to top managers' fear of negative feedback and to their implicit beliefs about employees, which produce structures such as centralized decisions and no upward-feedback mechanisms. (Seminal work, summarized from the established literature and not re-fetched in this session.) — [DOI 10.5465/amr.2000.3707697](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2000.3707697)
- **[S][PR] Milliken, Morrison & Hewlin (2003), J. Management Studies.** In interviews with 40 employees, **34 of 40 (85%)** said they had at least once felt unable to raise an important issue. Reasons given:
  - own lack of experience or rank: 32.5%
  - fear of being seen as a troublemaker or complainer: 30%
  - hierarchical or unsupportive culture: 30%
  - fear of damaging relationships: 27.5%
  - **"a sense of futility": 25%**
  - fear of retaliation: 22.5%
  - unsupportive or distant supervisor: 20%

  Issues withheld included "organisational processes or performance, and **suggestions for improvement**" (35%) and concerns about a colleague's or supervisor's competence (37.5%). The study is qualitative with a small sample. — [Psych Safety Field Guide summary](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/milliken-morrison-hewlin-2003/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-6486.00387)
- **[S][PR] Detert & Burris (2007), AMJ ("Is the door really open?").** Sample: 3,149 employees and 223 managers in a restaurant chain. **Managerial openness** was more consistently related to voice than transformational leadership. Leader behavior had the strongest effect on voice among the **best-performing employees**. — [Field Guide](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/detert-burris-2007/). In a related study, only **51% of employees in a Fortune 100 company felt safe speaking up most of the time**. — [UT Austin news, 2009](https://news.utexas.edu/2009/09/07/companies-that-quiet-employee-opinions-alienate-workers-and-miss-out-on-big-ideas)
- **[PR] McClean, Burris & Detert (2013), AMJ 56(2) ("When does voice lead to exit? It depends on leadership").** Multisource data from 136 restaurants over two periods. Where managers lacked change orientation, participation in decisions, or access to resources, unit voice went with **higher** later turnover. Where managers had these, voice went with **lower** turnover. The authors' reasoning: when managers don't respond, employees conclude that speaking up is futile and lose faith that things will improve. — [U. Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/when-does-voice-lead-to-exit-it-depends-on-leadership/)
- **[PR] Detert, Burris, Harrison & Martin (2013), ASQ.** Voice directed to one's own leader helps unit outcomes when leaders are receptive and have power to act. Voice that flows *around* the leader, or is not acted on, can hurt. — [Field Guide](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/detert-burris-harrison-martin-2013/)
- **[PR] Sherf, Parke & Isaakyan (2021), AMJ.** Voice and silence are distinct constructs (meta-analytic r ≈ −.15 per secondary summary). **Perceived impact** relates more strongly to voice; **psychological safety** relates more strongly to silence. Silence is more strongly tied to burnout. The findings replicated in a six-month panel. Practical implication: making people feel safe is not enough to get ideas; they must also see that their ideas have impact. — [London Business School](https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/academic-research/d/distinguishing-voice-and-silence-at-work-unique-relationships-with-psychological-safety-impact-and-burnout); [Field Guide](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/sherf-parke-isaakyan-2021/)
- **[PR] Morrison (2023) review, "Employee voice and silence: taking stock a decade later."** Two findings matter here:
  - In a 33-country study, power distance predicts more *acquiescent* (resigned) and *prosocial* silence, but **not fear-based** silence.
  - Employees with more access to formal channels such as suggestion boxes used them more for helpful ideas, but became *more* selective about withholding anything that might disturb group harmony. Adding a channel can redirect candor rather than increase it.

  — [Field Guide summary](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/morrison-2023.md)
- **[S][PR] Edmondson (1999), ASQ.** Team psychological safety predicts learning behavior, including speaking up about errors and problems. (Seminal work, not re-fetched.) — [DOI 10.2307/2666999](https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999)
- **[S][PR] Detert & Edmondson (2011), AMJ, "Implicit voice theories".** Employees hold taken-for-granted self-censorship rules, for example "don't bypass the boss" and "don't embarrass the boss in public." These rules suppress voice even with supportive bosses. (Not re-fetched.) — [DOI 10.5465/amj.2011.61967925](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2011.61967925)
- **[S][PR] Burris (2012), AMJ.** Managers reacted less favorably to *challenging* voice than to *supportive* voice, both in performance ratings and in endorsement of the idea. (Not re-fetched.) — [DOI 10.5465/amj.2010.0562](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2010.0562)
- **[V] Gallup.** Management quality explains **70% of the variance** in team engagement. — [Gallup, Employee retention](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/649487/employee-retention.aspx)

### Inferences
- The owner's fear has strong empirical backing. Unanswered voice teaches people that speaking up is futile, and in units with unresponsive managers, voice becomes a leading indicator of exit (McClean et al., 2013).
- A tool can directly attack *futility*, which is the voice driver that product design can most influence: visible status, guaranteed response, visible outcomes. Fear-based silence needs leadership behavior plus confidentiality options; software alone won't fix it.
- The best performers are the most sensitive to manager openness (Detert & Burris, 2007). A system that "eats" ideas will lose the most valuable contributors first.

### Gaps
- I could not find a peer-reviewed study that quantifies the effect of a specific response time (for example, 24 hours vs. 2 weeks) on future voice in internal employee settings. The causal mechanism (futility) is well established; the exact dose-response is not.
- The Burris (2012), Edmondson (1999), Detert & Edmondson (2011) and Morrison & Milliken (2000) summaries come from the established literature and were not re-fetched in this session.

## 2. Classic suggestion-box failure modes

### Takeaway
Traditional suggestion boxes fail for consistent reasons: slow or no response, no feedback, central-committee bottlenecks, a focus on big "money-saving" ideas, and value-based cash awards. Best-in-class systems instead handle many small ideas quickly and locally, implement most of them, and recognize people rather than pay them.

### Cited Findings
- **[B] Robinson & Schroeder, *The Idea-Driven Organization* (2014).** Most companies "essentially just set up a suggestion box, which employees know from experience is where ideas go to die." The book has a chapter titled "The Electronic Suggestion Box Trap": putting the box online doesn't fix it. — [Berrett-Koehler book page](https://bkconnection.com/books/title/the-idea-driven-organization)
- **[B][M] Robinson, quoted in the New York Sun:** "When I see a suggestion box, I know the company is dead." The authors argue for pursuing **small ideas**. — [NY Sun](https://www.nysun.com/article/business-small-ideas-key-to-big-company-growth)
- **[B] Robinson & Schroeder, *Ideas Are Free* (2004), as summarized by the State of Colorado process-improvement office.** Organizations that give **few or no monetary awards** for ideas, or that reward good ideas company-wide, tend to do better at developing and using ideas. — [Colorado summary](https://sites.google.com/state.co.us/process-improvement/tools-resources/ideas-are-free)
- **[M] Reference summary on suggestion systems.** "Employees are unlikely to participate in the program if they experience a slow response, or no response, to their suggestions." — [Encyclopedia.com, Employee Suggestion Systems](https://www.encyclopedia.com/entrepreneurs/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/employee-suggestion-systems)
- **[V] Vendor blog summarizing 21 research pieces.** Says lack of timely feedback is the **#1 reason** employee idea programs fail. This is not peer-reviewed; treat as practitioner opinion. — [Disruptor League, 2016](https://disruptorleague.com/blog/2016/02/18/11-unexpected-facts-about-managing-employee-idea-programs)
- **[S][PR] Leach, Stride & Wood (2006), Int. J. Innovation Management.** Survey of 182 UK organizations (data 2000–01; 88 were ideasUK members). Two headline results:
  - **Decentralized** suggestion schemes and work-based systems were **more effective than centralized** and informal schemes, so local handling beats a central committee bottleneck.
  - Planning, publicity, feedback and management support each independently explained effectiveness, but **publicity and non-monetary rewards were "most decisive"**. Only **management support** made a unique contribution to the *implementation* rate (0.17 → 0.30, low vs. high support).

  — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/86742/) (full text read)
- **[PR] Piezunka & Dahlander (2019), AMJ, "Idea Rejected, Tie Formed".** Data: 70,159 organizations and 1,336,154 idea contributors on crowdsourcing platforms.
  - **Receiving a rejection *increased* newcomers' willingness to submit again** compared with hearing nothing.
  - The effect was stronger when the rejection **included an explanation**, and especially when the explanation matched the linguistic style of the original idea.

  The setting is external crowdsourcing, not employees, but the mechanism (silence is worse than "no") transfers. — [INSEAD](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/idea-rejected-tie-formed-organizations-feedback); [INSEAD Knowledge](https://digital.insead.edu/post/in-crowdsourcing-you-have-to-know-how-to-say-no-thanks/)
- **[B] Benchmarks from best-in-class systems (authors' own reporting).**
  - Brasilata (Brazil): **~150 ideas per person per year, >90% implemented**.
  - Clarion Hotel Stockholm: **67 implemented ideas per person per year**.

  — [Robinson & Schroeder in IA Magazine](https://iamagazine.com/viewpoints/bottom-up-unleash-the-power-of-employee-ideas)

### Inferences
Failure-mode checklist distilled from the sources:
1. **Black hole**: no acknowledgement and no status.
2. **Slow decisions** handled by a central committee (bottleneck).
3. **Rejection without explanation**, or no answer at all, which is worse than a reasoned "no."
4. **Big-idea bias**: valuing only ideas with large estimated savings, which makes evaluation slow and contested.
5. **Cash tied to estimated value**: invites disputes, slows evaluation, and makes ideas private property instead of team improvements.
6. **No publicity of outcomes**: nobody sees that anything happened.
7. **Low management support**: ideas are "accepted" but never implemented.

The Modus Trade situation (email, meetings and calls; "people think it's being handled") is failure mode #1 combined with #6, without even a box.

### Gaps
- Robinson & Schroeder's quantitative claims (for example "50–100+ ideas per employee per year") are the authors' own, not independently audited.
- The commonly cited "**24-hour acknowledgement / 72-hour decision**" rule attributed to Milliken & Co. surfaced only in a secondhand reader review found via search. I could not verify it in the book text. Present it as a practitioner heuristic, not a research finding.

## 3. Quantitative evidence: participation, implementation, "opinions count", and acting on feedback (including Russian and Kazakhstan data)

### Takeaway
Typical Western suggestion schemes get **well under one idea per employee per year** and implement a small fraction. Best-in-class lean systems get **tens of ideas per person, with 70–90%+ implemented**. Employee-survey data consistently show that **acting visibly on feedback matters far more than collecting it**: an "action gap" of roughly 40 percentage points separates HR's view from employees'. In Russia only about **11% of employees** have voiced any idea at work in three years.

### Cited Findings
**Participation and implementation in typical schemes vs. best in class**
- **[S][PR] Leach et al. (2006), 182 UK organizations, 2000–01.**
  - Ideas per covered employee: **median 0.13 per year** (mean 1.27).
  - Implementation rate: **median 0.02 (2%)**, mean 0.13.
  - Comparing schemes with low vs. high levels of each feature:

  | Feature | Suggestion rate, low | Suggestion rate, high |
  |---|---|---|
  | Feedback | 0.14 | 0.28 |
  | Frequently updated publicity | 0.14 | 0.59 |
  | Non-monetary rewards | 0.16 | 0.46 |

  — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/86742/)
- **[M] Employee Involvement Association 2003 study of 47 companies.** About **32% of employees submitted at least one idea**, and ideas saved more than $624M. (Snippet; the SHRM page did not load.) — [SHRM HR Magazine, 2005](https://www.shrm.org/hr-today/news/hr-magazine/Pages/0205wells.aspx)
- **[M] 1997 survey of US manufacturers.** About **0.5 suggestions per employee per year**. — [Reference for Business](https://www.referenceforbusiness.com/small/Di-Eq/Employee-Suggestion-Systems.html)
- **[M] Secondhand estimates for the "average US employee".**
  - One idea **implemented once every six years** (attributed to Robinson research reported in the WSJ). — [GovLoop](https://govloop.com/community/blog/how-did-i-hire-so-many-people-who-have-no-ideas)
  - One formal idea submitted **every 8 years**, with fewer than a third implemented. — [Lean Blog guest post, 2010](https://leanblog.org/2010/07/guest-post-the-hidden-gold-on-the-frontlines)
  - Both figures are unverified.
- **[S][M] Japan vs. US, via a secondary source.**
  - Womack, Jones & Roos (1990): Japanese automakers averaged **61.8 ideas per employee per year** vs. **0.4** in US and European plants.
  - A cross-country comparison: Japan **32.5 ideas per employee, 87% implemented, 72% participation** vs. US **0.11 ideas, 32% implemented**. The year of the data is unclear.

  — [Gemba Academy blog](https://blog.gembaacademy.com/?p=38) (secondary; verify against the originals)
- **[S] Toyota.**
  - 1973: more than 250,000 suggestions from 43,000 employees, with more than 70% adopted. — [Toyota press release, 1973](https://global.toyota/en/detail/7756353)
  - 2023: about **810,000 suggestions, ~14.4 per person**. (Snippet.) — [Toyota Times](https://toyotatimes.jp/en/series/Imaginative_and_creative/001_1.html)

**"My opinions count" (Gallup)**
- **[V] Gallup Q12, item Q07 "At work, my opinions seem to count."** Globally **1 in 4 employees strongly agree**. Gallup estimates that **doubling** that ratio could give a **22% reduction in turnover, 33% fewer safety incidents and 10% higher productivity**. — [Gallup Q12 question summary](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/356045/q12-question-summary.aspx). Other Gallup editions give different projections (for example, a 27% / 40% / 12% version for moving to 6 in 10). Cite one edition only.
- **[V] Gallup State of the Global Workplace 2026 (data Jan–Dec 2025), Kazakhstan.**
  - **23% engaged**, 67% not engaged, 11% actively disengaged.
  - Post-Soviet Eurasia regional average: 25%. Global average: 20%.

  — [Gallup Kazakhstan country page](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/705842/state-global-workplace-kazakhstan-country-level-data.aspx)

**Acting on feedback matters more than collecting it**
- **[V] Qualtrics XM Institute (2023).** **84% of HR leaders** (n = 911, nine countries) rate their organization's use of employee feedback as effective, but only **43% of employees** (n = 16,163) say they have seen positive change from previous surveys: a **41-point gap**. — [Qualtrics](https://www.qualtrics.com/articles/employee-experience/hr-execs-overestimate-feedback/). In a 2024 study of more than 35,000 employees, only about half of individual contributors believed their company had made positive changes based on their feedback. (Snippet, same source family.)
- **[V] Perceptyx benchmark database (12M+ responses, article dated May 2021).** Organizations that surveyed but **did not act** vs. those that **acted**:

  | Outcome | Did not act | Acted |
  |---|---|---|
  | Engagement increased over three years | 8% | 74% |
  | Scored above the 50th percentile on engagement | 25% | 78% |

  The same article cites Rotolo, Fleck & Shepherd (2020): action planning is the biggest pain point for **52%** of organizations. — [Perceptyx](https://blog.perceptyx.com/are-employee-surveys-effective)
- **[V] Perceptyx on "survey fatigue".** It argues that "there's really no such thing as survey fatigue; however, **inaction fatigue** plagues organizations." In one client case, response rates rose noticeably when the same group was resurveyed three weeks after visible changes; no figures were given. — [Perceptyx](https://blog.perceptyx.com/theres-no-such-thing-as-survey-fatigue-when-you-act-on-employee-feedback). A later benchmark found that 71% of organizations share survey results, but only 51% of employees see tangible improvements. (Snippet.) — [Perceptyx](https://blog.perceptyx.com/multi-channel-employee-listening-grows-but-action-struggles-persist)

**Russian-language data**
- **[PR-adjacent: official research institute] HSE ISSEK (НИУ ВШЭ ИСИЭЗ), bulletin of 19.03.2025, "Идеи сотрудников как инновационный ресурс организации".** Monitoring survey 2023/24, 4,418 employees out of 6,946 respondents.
  - Only **11% of employed Russians** voiced any idea at work in the past three years.
  - Ideas concerned improving goods and services (44%), processes (38%) and creating something new (32%).
  - **52%** say their organization has created conditions for voicing ideas.
  - Organizations stimulate ideas mostly through **non-monetary** means.

  — [ИСИЭЗ НИУ ВШЭ](https://issek.hse.ru/mirror/pubs/share/1026160741.pdf). An earlier HSE estimate from 2018 data was about **7%**. — [Вопросы экономики](https://www.vopreco.ru/jour/article/view/3276)
- **[V] SuperJob (1,600 employees, as reported by Tochka).**
  - **63%** view colleagues' initiatives positively; 4% negatively.
  - The share who see managers encouraging initiative rose from **40% to 49%** in two years.
  - Support from colleagues rose from 34% (2023) to 41% (2025).

  — [allo.tochka.com](https://allo.tochka.com/news/initsiativa-collegi)
- **[V][M] hh.ru (2,800+ respondents, via secondary source).** 43% can't name anything attractive about their manager. Only **6%** named "ability to implement new things" as a manager strength. — [Т-Бизнес секреты](https://secrets.tbank.ru/novosti/plokhiye-nachalniki/)
- **[M] Blog claim: "73% of Russian employees are afraid to disagree with management" (HeadHunter, 2026).** Found only in a secondary blog; the primary hh.ru release was not found. **Do not cite without verification.** — [Sostav blog](https://www.sostav.ru/blogs/288536/85868)
- **[V] hh.kz (Kazakhstan, 730+ users, June 2025).** Complaints include "difficult management style" (28%) and "toxic atmosphere" (25%). "Respectful treatment" is important to 41%. — [Bizmedia.kz](https://bizmedia.kz/2025-06-17-chto-vdohnovlyaet-a-chto-vymatyvaet-na-rabote-opros-hh-kz/)

### Inferences
- Pitch framing: "Typical idea schemes: ~0.13 ideas per employee per year, ~2% implemented (UK study, 182 organizations). Best-in-class: tens of ideas per person, 70–90%+ implemented. The difference is process, not people."
- The Qualtrics 84% vs. 43% gap and the Perceptyx 78% vs. 25% comparison are the strongest *vendor* numbers for "closing the loop matters." Label them as vendor data.
- The Russian baseline (11% have ever voiced an idea in three years) suggests large untapped potential in post-Soviet workplaces.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed, quantified dose-response between **response time** and **participation** for internal systems.
- I found no Kazakhstan-specific survey on employee ideas or voice. The hh.kz data is only adjacent.
- The Gallup US figure ("3 in 10 strongly agree opinions count") appears in Gallup materials, but I could not pin the exact page and edition in this session.
- No Culture Amp figure was retrieved.

## 4. Anonymity: candor vs. follow-up, and confidential vs. anonymous

### Takeaway
Anonymity increases candor, and in many regions most hotline reporters choose it. But anonymous reporters rarely come back to answer follow-up questions, and anonymous reports are harder to substantiate and slower to close. Well-run systems offer a **graded choice (named, confidential, anonymous)** and give anonymous reporters a **two-way channel** (case key or token).

### Cited Findings
- **[V] NAVEX whistleblowing benchmark (2025 report, 2024 data).**
  - Median share of anonymous reports: **54% globally**; 65% Europe, 67% APAC, 70% South America, 52% North America.
  - Overall median substantiation rate: **46%**, an all-time high.

  — [NAVEX regional key findings PDF](https://cdn.navex.com/image/upload/v1748609318/resources-2025/benchmarking-reports/navex-2025-regional-bmk-whistleblowing-key-findings_US_EN.pdf); [NAVEX press release](https://www.navex.com/en-us/company/press-room/regional-whistleblowing-benchmark-report-highlights-rise-in-anonymous-reports/)
- **[V] NAVEX: follow-up by anonymous reporters.** The global median rate at which anonymous reporters return to follow up was reported as **26% in 2024, down from 36% in 2019** (snippet via JD Supra). Another NAVEX source describes follow-up engagement as a "five-year high." **The sources conflict**; check the PDF before citing. — [JD Supra summary](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/navex-s-2025-hotline-benchmark-report-5585031); [NAVEX 2025 report PDF](https://cdn.navex.com/image/upload/v1742410133/resources-2025/benchmarking-reports/2025-navex-whistleblowing-incident-mgt-benchmark-report.pdf)
- **[V] NAVEX: closure times and channel differences.**
  - Median days to close: about 69 in Europe, 56 in APAC, 19–26 in North America. Named reports generally close faster. (Snippet.)
  - Older data: the anonymous-report substantiation rate held at **36%** (2013–2015). — [NAVEX 2016 benchmark](https://www.reit.com/sites/default/files/Foosel_2016_Hotline_BenchmarkReport_attachement.pdf)
  - Web reports were substantiated more often than phone reports (39% vs. 33%), probably because web filers have time to frame their concerns. — [JD Supra/NAVEX](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/hotline-report-substantiation-4726515)
- **[M] Compliance Week.** Anonymous complaints are harder to investigate and substantiate, so robust **follow-up communication procedures** matter especially for them. — [Compliance Week](https://www.complianceweek.com/from-the-archive/best-practices-for-maintaining-a-healthy-incident-response-program-2/)
- **[S][PR] Antonioni (1994), Personnel Psychology, on upward appraisal.**
  - When raters were **identifiable**, their ratings of managers were **higher (inflated)** than when anonymous.
  - Managers preferred identified feedback; raters preferred anonymity.
  - London & Wohlers (1991): up to **24%** of subordinates said they would rate their manager differently if feedback were not anonymous.

  (Via a secondary summary.) — [J. Management Research summary](https://ajol.info/index.php/ajmr/article/view/117986/107603)
- **[PR] Milliken et al. (2003).** The most frequently withheld issue type was concerns about a **colleague's or supervisor's competence** (37.5%). This is the category where identified channels fail. — [Field Guide](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/milliken-morrison-hewlin-2003/)

### Inferences
- For routine *ideas and operational problems* (most Modus Trade traffic), **named by default** is better: the owner can ask clarifying questions, the submitter gets credit, and recognition is possible.
- For *sensitive* issues (about a manager, a colleague, or ethics), offer:
  - **Confidential**: identity visible only to a designated owner or admin, not to the line manager.
  - **Anonymous**: no identity stored, but with an **anonymous reply thread** so that follow-up stays possible. The low anonymous follow-up rates in hotline data show why the product must push status updates to the reporter rather than wait for them to come back.
- Expect higher demand for anonymity in Central Asia than in North America. This is inferred from the regional anonymity pattern (Europe, APAC and South America above 65%) plus high power distance, not from Kazakhstan-specific data.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous study comparing *anonymous vs. named idea/suggestion systems* (as opposed to hotlines or 360 reviews) on idea quality or participation.
- I found no data on the misuse rate (malicious or abusive submissions) in anonymous internal idea channels.

## 5. Recognition vs. monetary rewards for sustained participation

### Takeaway
Evidence favors **recognition and non-monetary rewards** for sustained participation. Large, value-based cash awards show little benefit and create known problems: disputes, slow evaluation, and private ownership of ideas. Modest rewards tied to *implementation* can broaden participation without crowding out motivation.

### Cited Findings
- **[S][PR] Leach, Stride & Wood (2006), 182 UK organizations.**
  - Recognition and non-monetary rewards were significantly related to perceived success and suggestion rate.
  - **Monetary rewards were unrelated to both suggestion and implementation rates** (p > .05); they related only weakly to perceived success.
  - With all rewards modelled together, only **non-monetary rewards** uniquely predicted suggestion rate.
  - The authors' summary: "There is less evidence than expected of the beneficial effect of monetary rewards."

  — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/86742/)
- **[PR] Gibbs, Neckermann & Siemroth (2017), Review of Economics & Statistics.** Randomized field experiment across 19 account teams at a large technology firm, testing rewards for *approved/implemented* ideas.
  - **Participation increased**, but ideas per participating employee fell, so **total quantity was unchanged**.
  - **Idea quality rose**.
  - Broader participation **persisted after the reward was withdrawn**.
  - There was **no motivational crowding-out**.

  — [IZA DP 8096](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/8096); [IZA Newsroom](https://newsroom.iza.org/en/archive/research/how-to-make-your-employees-creative-evidence-from-a-field-experiment)
- **[B] Robinson & Schroeder.** Organizations with **few or no monetary awards**, or with company-wide reward sharing, tend to do better. — [Colorado summary of *Ideas Are Free*](https://sites.google.com/state.co.us/process-improvement/tools-resources/ideas-are-free)
- **[PR-adjacent] HSE ISSEK 2025.** Russian organizations that stimulate employee ideas do so mostly through **non-monetary** means. — [ИСИЭЗ НИУ ВШЭ](https://issek.hse.ru/mirror/pubs/share/1026160741.pdf)
- **[PR] Sherf et al. (2021).** Perceived impact drives voice, so the most powerful "reward" is seeing the idea implemented. — [LBS](https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/academic-research/d/distinguishing-voice-and-silence-at-work-unique-relationships-with-psychological-safety-impact-and-burnout)

### Inferences
- For an MVP: **recognition first**. Visible attribution on implemented items, a thank-you from the owner, and a monthly "implemented ideas" digest. Any prize should be **small, symbolic and tied to implementation**, not to estimated monetary value.
- The Gibbs et al. finding that rewards broaden participation suggests a light gamification layer (for example, a team leaderboard of *implemented* items) could help. That is an inference, not a tested design.

### Gaps
- I found no head-to-head trial of *recognition-only vs. cash* in an employee idea system.
- Gibbs et al. compared rewards against no reward, not against recognition.

## 6. Metrics that well-run programs track, with benchmarks

### Takeaway
Well-run programs track **speed** (time to acknowledgement and decision), **throughput** (ideas per employee, participation rate), **conversion** (implementation rate) and **outcome** (realized value, closure with feedback). Published benchmarks span about two orders of magnitude between typical and best-in-class programs.

- MVP metrics in priority order:
  1. Median **time to first human response**
  2. Median **time to decision**
  3. **% of items closed with an explanation** sent to the submitter
  4. **% overdue** (past SLA)
  5. **Implementation rate**
  6. **Participation rate** (% of staff with at least one submission per quarter)
  7. **Ideas/issues per employee per year**
  8. **Repeat-submitter rate** (a direct proxy for "not giving up"; Piezunka & Dahlander use resubmission as the outcome)

- Realistic targets for a ~50–200-person distributor in year one: beat the UK median of 0.13 ideas per employee by 5–10x; get participation above 30% (the EIA figure); keep the 2% implementation trap far away. These targets are inferred, not benchmarks.

## 7. Cultural context: post-Soviet, Central Asian and high power-distance workplaces

### Takeaway
Kazakhstan and Russia are consistently described as **high power distance** cultures (autocratic style, authority by position, little delegation). In such cultures, formal voice channels raise voice **only when employees also perceive a genuinely participative climate**. Silence in high power-distance cultures tends to be *acquiescent or prosocial* ("not my place," "keep harmony") rather than fearful. This argues for owner-visible responsiveness, confidential options, and allowing managers or peers to submit on someone's behalf.

### Cited Findings
- **[S][PR] Huang, Van de Vliert & Van der Vegt (2005), Management and Organization Review.** Study of 421 units of a multinational across 24 countries.
  - In **low power-distance** countries, both formal employee involvement and participative climate reduced opinion withholding.
  - In **high power-distance** countries, **formal involvement related to voice only under a strong perceived participative climate**.

  — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/maorev/v1y2005i03p459-482_00.html)
- **[PR] Morrison (2023) review.** In a 33-country study, power distance predicts **acquiescent and prosocial silence**, not fear-based silence. — [Field Guide](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/morrison-2023.md)
- **[M/academic review] Papla, Kydyr & Pak (2022), Universum.** Kazakhstan is characterized as high power distance (autocratic style, position-based authority, little delegation, citing Muratbekova-Touron 2002) and high uncertainty avoidance (strict rules, bureaucracy, centralization). The culture was historically collectivist and is shifting toward individualism. The review notes low involvement of employees in decision-making. No numeric Hofstede scores are given. — [7universum.com](https://7universum.com/ru/economy/archive/item/13584)
- **[PR-adjacent] HSE ISSEK (2025).** Only **11%** of Russian employees voiced an idea at work in three years; 52% say conditions for voicing exist. — [ИСИЭЗ НИУ ВШЭ](https://issek.hse.ru/mirror/pubs/share/1026160741.pdf)
- **[V] SuperJob (Russia).** The share of employees seeing managers encourage initiative is rising (40% → 49%), which suggests an improving climate. — [allo.tochka.com](https://allo.tochka.com/news/initsiativa-collegi)
- **[V] Gallup 2026 (2025 data).** Kazakhstan engagement is 23%, against 25% in Post-Soviet Eurasia and 20% globally. Daily stress (14%) and anger (10%) are well below global averages (40% and 22%). — [Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/705842/state-global-workplace-kazakhstan-country-level-data.aspx)
- **[S][PR] Detert & Edmondson (2011).** "Don't bypass the boss" is a common implicit voice rule. It is likely stronger in high power-distance settings (inference). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2011.61967925)

### Inferences
- In a high power-distance, owner-led company, **the owner's visible behavior is the participative climate**. Huang et al. imply that a tool alone (formal involvement) won't raise voice unless people see the owner and managers actually responding. The first weeks of responses are decisive.
- Acquiescent silence ("not my place to say," "the boss knows") is best countered by **explicit invitation** (targeted asks such as "What slows down installations on site?") and **low-status-friendly capture**: field technicians speaking to a bot, or a manager logging the issue on their behalf.
- **Manager-mediated submission with attribution preserved** fits the "don't bypass the boss" norm. A **confidential direct-to-owner path** is still needed for issues *about* the manager (37.5% of withheld issues in Milliken et al. concern colleague or supervisor competence).

### Gaps
- I found **no official Hofstede Insights score for Kazakhstan**. Secondary sources found via search cite power distance ≈ 88 and uncertainty avoidance ≈ 88, but I could not trace them to an authoritative dataset. Russia's commonly cited power distance score (93) was **not verified** in this session.
- I found no peer-reviewed study of *employee voice or suggestion systems specifically in Kazakhstan or Central Asia*.
- I found no data on the Soviet BRIZ (rationalization proposals) legacy and how it shapes current attitudes. This could be a useful local hook but needs research.

## Design implications for our MVP

Numbered product rules, each tied to the evidence above.

1. **One intake, many doors.** Accept items from a web form, a messenger bot (Telegram/WhatsApp), email forwarding, and **"log on behalf of"** (a manager records a remark from a meeting or call and credits the original person). Every item gets a unique ID. *Evidence:* the chaotic multi-channel intake is failure mode #1 (black hole); work-based and decentralized capture beat centralized and informal schemes (Leach 2006); proxy logging fits high power-distance norms (Huang 2005; Detert & Edmondson 2011).
2. **Instant receipt, then a human acknowledgement SLA.** Send an automatic confirmation within seconds, and a named human acknowledgement within **1 business day** (configurable). *Evidence:* slow or no response is the top failure reason (Encyclopedia.com; practitioner literature); futility drives silence (Milliken 2003; McClean 2013). The 24h/72h rule is a practitioner heuristic, labelled unverified.
3. **Every item has exactly one owner and a due date.** No ownerless items. Unassigned items escalate after N hours. *Evidence:* "employees think it's being handled but it gets silently forgotten"; management support is the only unique predictor of implementation rate (Leach 2006: 0.17 → 0.30).
4. **A visible status lifecycle for the submitter:** Received → Assigned → In progress → Decision (Implemented / Planned / Declined / Parked). Push every status change to the submitter. *Evidence:* perceived impact drives voice (Sherf 2021); anonymous and other reporters rarely check back on their own (NAVEX follow-up rate around 26–36%), so updates must be pushed.
5. **No silent closure. Closing requires a written reason sent to the submitter**, with a "Declined because…" template that encourages a personal reply. *Evidence:* a rejection with explanation increases resubmission compared with no response (Piezunka & Dahlander 2019, 1.3M contributors).
6. **SLA breaches escalate automatically** to the owner's manager, and then to the company owner. The owner sees an "overdue" dashboard. *Evidence:* voice leads to exit when managers can't or won't act (McClean 2013); manager openness matters most for top performers (Detert & Burris 2007).
7. **"You said, we did" feed and a monthly digest** of implemented items, crediting the submitters. *Evidence:* frequently updated publicity went with a 4x higher suggestion rate (0.59 vs. 0.14; Leach 2006); the action gap of 84% vs. 43% (Qualtrics 2023); 78% vs. 25% of organizations above median engagement when they act vs. don't (Perceptyx, vendor).
8. **Favor small, fast, local fixes.** Add a "quick win" flag, let the submitter or team implement small items themselves, and route each item to the person closest to the problem rather than a central committee. *Evidence:* decentralized schemes outperform centralized ones (Leach 2006); best-in-class systems are built on many small ideas (Robinson & Schroeder: Brasilata ~150 per person with >90% implemented).
9. **Recognition, not cash.** Show attribution on implemented items, a one-click owner "thank you," and team counts of *implemented* items. If prizes exist, keep them small and tied to implementation, never to estimated value. *Evidence:* monetary rewards were unrelated to suggestion and implementation rates while non-monetary rewards predicted them (Leach 2006); implementation-tied rewards broadened participation with no crowding-out (Gibbs 2017); Robinson & Schroeder.
10. **Three identity modes:**
    - **Named** (default, for ideas and operational issues).
    - **Confidential** (identity visible only to the designated owner or admin, hidden from the line manager).
    - **Anonymous** (nothing stored, but a **secret-link reply thread** so follow-up remains possible).

    *Evidence:* anonymity increases candor (Antonioni 1994; London & Wohlers: 24% would rate differently); anonymous reports are the majority in many regions (NAVEX: 54% global, 65–70% Europe/APAC/South America); anonymous follow-up is weak (NAVEX); 37.5% of withheld issues concern colleague or supervisor competence (Milliken 2003).
11. **A sensitive-topic route that bypasses the line manager** (confidential, direct to the owner or HR) while normal items flow through the manager. *Evidence:* "don't bypass the boss" norms (Detert & Edmondson 2011); voice around the leader can hurt when routine (Detert et al. 2013), but issues *about* the leader need another path.
12. **Show the system's own track record to fight futility.** Display "median first response: X h," "% answered with a reason: Y%," and on each person's profile "N of your items implemented." *Evidence:* perceived impact is the main driver of voice (Sherf 2021); futility is a named silence reason (25% in Milliken 2003).
13. **Targeted prompts instead of generic surveys.** Run periodic one-question asks ("What slowed down your last installation?") and **don't collect what you won't act on**. *Evidence:* "inaction fatigue," not survey fatigue (Perceptyx); acquiescent silence in high power-distance cultures needs explicit invitation (Morrison 2023; Huang 2005).
14. **A built-in metrics panel** (the MVP's KPI set):
    - time to first response
    - time to decision
    - % closed with an explanation
    - % overdue
    - implementation rate
    - participation rate
    - ideas per employee per year
    - repeat-submitter rate
    - optional realized value

    Baselines to beat: UK median of 0.13 ideas per employee per year and 2% implemented (Leach 2006); EIA participation of ~32%.
15. **Make leadership behavior part of the launch.** The owner announces the tool, responds personally to the first batch, and publishes the first "we did" digest within 2 weeks. *Evidence:* formal channels raise voice in high power-distance cultures only under a perceived participative climate (Huang 2005); management quality explains 70% of engagement variance (Gallup).
16. **Separate item types with different routing and SLAs:** Problem/Issue, Idea/Improvement, Question, Sensitive concern. *Evidence:* different issue types carry different silence risks (Milliken 2003); hotline-style concerns need investigation workflows and longer timelines (NAVEX), while ideas need fast yes/no.
